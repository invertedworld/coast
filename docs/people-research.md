# People in Coast: research and a first testbench

This note sets out how townspeople might be added to Coast. They are to be low in polygons, varied in appearance and natural in behaviour. They must walk, sit and look about, keep clear of one another and walk in groups, and they must include children. It compares the ways in which the bodies may be made and animated, summarises the research on how pedestrians behave, and describes the first prototype, `testbench/people.html`. It ends with a plan for bringing people into the game, and the faults that are still open.

The game once placed static figures (`addFigure`, now an empty function in `index.html`) at café counters, in gardens and on terraces. Those call sites remain, and they mark places where people would naturally be found.

## 1. Bodies and animation

Three approaches were considered.

### 1.1 Procedural rigid segments, posed on the GPU (chosen for the prototype)

Each person is fourteen rigid segments: the pelvis, torso, head, both upper arms and forearms, both thighs, shins and feet, and a skirt. Each segment is a short faceted loft of six to ten sides, built in code in its own frame with its joint at the origin. All people share one geometry and are drawn as one `InstancedMesh`. Every frame, the matrix of each segment is computed in JavaScript and written into a float texture of one row per person. The vertex shader reads the matrix of its segment by `gl_InstanceID` and the segment index, so the whole crowd, and its shadow pass, costs a single draw call. The same method already poses the gulls' wings.

- **Variety without extra geometry.** Each triangle carries a *role* and a *part*. The role chooses one of thirteen colours per person: skin, hair, top, bottom, shoes, hat, the lower thigh, the shin, the upper and lower arm, the eyes, the skirt and a bag. A short sleeve is therefore the lower arm given the colour of the skin, and shorts are the shin given the skin. The part is a bit that the person's mask must contain for the triangle to be drawn. The parts are short hair, long hair, a bun, a balding fringe, a flat cap, a straw hat, a headscarf, a knee-length or mid-calf skirt, a shopping bag and a bag on a strap. Proportions are set per person by scale: height, girth, shoulder and hip width, and, for children, a larger head, shorter legs and larger feet. Older people stoop.
- **Cost.** About 1,370 triangles per person. With 140 people this is about 190,000 triangles in one draw call. The texture is 56 texels wide, one row per person.
- **Licence.** Everything is generated by the game's own code, so nothing needs to be licensed or credited.
- **Limits.** Rigid segments cannot bend at a joint, so elbows, knees and the waist show small gaps or overlaps at close range. Clothing cannot drape. Faces are a nose, two eyes and two ears.

A post on the three.js forum, "One Draw Call, Massive Crowd", describes essentially the same technique (merged rigid parts tagged with a part number and posed in the shader) and reports crowds far larger than Coast needs. No licence is given for its code, which was not used.

### 1.2 Skinned glTF characters

A skinned mesh is a continuous surface bent smoothly over a skeleton of 20 to 65 bones, with key-framed animations. It looks better at close range, but costs a skinned draw call per character unless the skinning is instanced. Instanced skinning needs either a library such as InstancedMesh2 (`@three.ez/instanced-mesh`, MIT licence), whose current release requires three.js 0.186 or later where Coast uses r160, or bone matrices baked into a texture.

A search of GitHub and the usual free sources found the following. Where a licence or a figure was not confirmed from an official page or licence file, this is said.

Its benefit is smoother joints at close range.

### 1.3 Baked vertex-animation textures

A clip is baked into a texture of vertex positions per frame, and the shader plays it back for each instance. Crowds of thousands are cheap this way, but every pose must be authored in advance. Turning the head towards a target, keeping a foot planted on a slope, or blending between sitting and standing at an arbitrary seat height is then difficult. The behaviours listed in section 2 depend on exactly those things. The bakers that exist are for Blender (OpenVAT, GPL-3.0, usable as a tool but not as code in this repository) and for Godot (MIT). three.js r160 also offers `InstancedMesh.setMorphAt`, which could drive a few baked key poses.

### 1.4 Recommendation

The procedural approach should be kept for the crowd. It needs no assets or licences, varies freely, poses any seat height, slope or gaze at run time, and costs one draw call. If the close views of a few people prove too crude, a skinned body driven by the same pose numbers (section 3) could be used for the nearest half-dozen, with Mesh2Motion's CC0 clips as reference for the motion.

## 2. How pedestrians behave

The prototype follows these findings.

- **Speed and gait.** Adults walk at about 1.34 m/s on average, with a spread of roughly ±0.2 m/s (Weidmann, 1993). Women and older people walk more slowly; older people walk at about 1.0 m/s. Step length grows with speed and with leg length; at normal speed it is about 0.4 of body height, which gives about two steps a second. Children therefore take shorter, quicker steps, and run often.
- **Groups.** A large share of pedestrians walk in groups, up to about 70 per cent in a shopping street. A group walks more slowly than a single walker, and the more slowly the larger it is. At low density its members walk abreast. As density rises, a group of three or four bends into a V or U with the middle members slightly behind, which keeps everyone in conversation. In a narrow passage it folds into a file. Members also turn their heads towards one another as they talk (Moussaïd, Perozo, Garnier, Helbing and Theraulaz, *PLoS ONE* 5(4), 2010).
- **Avoiding one another.** People react to the expected *time to collision* rather than to distance. Their interaction energy falls off with roughly the inverse square of that time, almost independently of how far apart they are (Karamouzas, Skinner and Guy, *Physical Review Letters* 113, 2014). This gives the anticipatory, early sidestep seen in real streets, which distance-based models such as the social force model (Helbing and Molnár, *Physical Review E* 51, 1995) do not. People also tend to keep to one side when meeting head on; in Italy, as in most of Europe, that side is the right.
- **Steering and paths.** Reynolds' steering behaviours (GDC, 1999) are the usual basis for seeking a target, arriving and following a path. Distance fields over a grid, or flow fields (Treuille, Cooper and Popović, *Continuum Crowds*, 2006), give each walker a way round obstacles towards a shared goal at little cost.
- **Standing together.** A conversing group stands in an F-formation: a ring around a shared space that its members face (Kendon, 1990). Two people stand at about a metre, within the personal distance of 0.45 to 1.2 m (Hall, 1966). One person speaks at a time; the others look at the speaker most of the time, with glances elsewhere.
- **Looking about.** Gaze moves in quick shifts and is held for one to a few seconds. Walkers mostly look along their path and at the ground a few metres ahead. Moving things catch the eye, particularly people approaching and running children. A person who is close and looking at someone is usually looked back at. The head turns first, then the shoulders, and, if the target stays far to the side, the whole body.
- **Daily rhythm.** In a Mediterranean town the streets are busy in the morning, nearly empty in the hours after lunch, and fullest during the evening walk (the *passeggiata*). Older people sit outside for long periods.

## 3. The prototype: `testbench/people.html`

The page is served from the repository root like the other testbenches, and uses the game's palette, sky, lights, fog and course of the sun. Its scene is a piazza of 44 by 34 m with a fountain, two trees in round planters, nine benches, a café of five tables and houses with doors and windows, and four streets that lead out of it. The panel offers:

- five views: from above, at street level, following one person (a click on anyone also follows them), from a bench, and a line-up of 24 people in two rows;
- for the line-up, five poses: standing, walking, running, sitting and talking;
- the number of people, the share of children and the share of people in company;
- the time of day, a pause, slow motion, new faces (a new seed), an overlay of velocities, paths, formations and gazes, and an automatic turn of the view.

The query string sets the same values, for example `?v=lineup&pose=sit` or `?n=140&kids=30`.

### 3.1 Pose

`buildPose` makes the pose of one person from a few numbers: the smoothed speed, the gait phase, how far the legs walk rather than stand, stepping round on the spot, how far the person is seated, how the arms are held, and the head's yaw and pitch.

- **Walking and running.** The hip swing is the angle that gives the step length for that leg length at that speed. The knee bends in the swing phase and the arms swing against the legs. The pelvis turns and the shoulders counter-turn. Above about 2 m/s the knees and elbows bend further, the body leans forward and the arms pump.
- **Feet on the ground.** The pelvis is set at the height at which the lower foot meets the ground, so the bob and the dip at each footfall follow from the legs. The foot is kept level and lifts its heel at toe-off.
- **Standing.** The weight shifts slowly from one leg to the other, with the free knee relaxed.
- **Arms.** They may be held behind the back (some older men while walking), folded or on the hips (listeners), gesturing (the speaker), reaching out and down to hold a hand (a child and the adult beside it), or straight down holding a shopping bag.
- **Sitting.** The thighs are level at the seat's height. The shins reach the ground at an angle found from the seat's height, or hang and swing if they are too short, as a child's do. Some sitters cross a leg, rest their hands in their lap, or lean forward with their forearms on their knees. The skirt follows the thighs. Sitting down and standing up take about a second: the person turns to face away from the seat and lowers onto it.
- **Gaze.** The head turns towards its target quickly and the shoulders follow a little. The head's pitch makes up for the lean of the body, so that a stooping person still looks ahead.

### 3.2 The crowd

- **Groups.** Everyone belongs to a group of one to five: single walkers, couples, friends, older pairs, families (one or two adults, one to three children, sometimes a grandparent) and pairs or threes of older children. The mix follows the panel's shares.
- **Anchor and formation.** A group has a destination and a virtual anchor that walks there along a flow field, at the pace of its slowest adult reduced for the group's size. The anchor waits when its members fall behind. Each member steers for a slot round the anchor: abreast for two, a shallow U for three or more, and rows of as many as fit where the street's clearance is too small. Children walk between their parents. From time to time a child runs a few metres ahead or takes a parent's hand.
- **Paths.** The walkable ground is a grid of 0.5 m cells holding the clearance to the nearest wall or obstacle. A path is a Dijkstra distance field to the target's cell over cells with enough clearance, made a little dearer close to walls and cached by target. A walker follows its steepest descent and steers for the furthest point of it that is in clear view, so that paths across open ground are straight. At most two new fields are made in a frame.
- **Avoidance.** Walkers avoid one another by the time-to-collision force of Karamouzas et al. The force is weaker between members of one group, and a small bias to the right is added when meeting someone head on. Overlaps are pushed apart, and the clearance grid holds people off walls. People standing still are not pushed by approaching walkers, who go round them.
- **Activities.** On arrival a group sits (a bench, the rim of the fountain, a planter's ring, or a café table not shared with strangers), looks into a window or at the fountain, stops to talk in a ring facing its middle, pauses and looks about, or goes in at a door or leaves by the end of a street. Seats are reserved when they are chosen. Two groups that pass in the piazza now and then stop and talk together. In a talking ring one person speaks at a time and gestures, and the others mostly look at the speaker.
- **Children's games.** While their family is stopped, children may play tag with any children close by. The child who is "it" chases the nearest, the others flee within reach of home, and a child who is caught stands still for a moment and becomes "it".
- **Coming and going.** New groups come out of doors and from the ends of the streets when the number of people falls below the setting. When it rises above it, groups are sent home.

## 4. Bringing people into the game

1. **Where they live.** People belong to the blocks of villages, harbours, stations and beaches, as the cats do. A block's people are kept in a state table while the player is within a few blocks, so that someone left behind is where they were when the player returns. Up to about 150 of those nearest the camera are posed and drawn. Those further away are advanced at a lower rate, and not drawn beyond the fog.
2. **Seats and places.** Seats are already recorded per block (`ch.seats`, `addSeat`). People can use them directly, reserving a seat so that a person and the player do not take it together; **X** should then refuse a seat that is occupied. The old `addFigure` call sites mark the café counters, the gardens and the terraces. Doors are known where houses are built.
3. **The ground.** The walkable surface (`heightAt`, `walkGround`, the steps and terraces) gives each person's height. On steps and steep alleys the stride must be fitted to the treads, and each foot placed on the surface beneath it. This needs a small two-bone leg solver, which the prototype does not yet have.
4. **Obstacles and paths.** A block's oriented boxes (`ch.obbs`), trees and walls can be rasterised into a clearance grid when the block is built, at the level of detail of its full geometry. The flow fields can then be computed per block, with links across block edges at the alleys. Squares and harbour quays are open enough for the line-of-sight steering to do most of the work.
5. **The player.** People should treat the player as another walker in the avoidance, glance at the player when close, and occasionally greet them with a nod. The cats are obstacles of their own size.
6. **Time of day.** The number of people out, and what they do, should follow the hour: a busy morning market, empty streets after lunch, the evening walk on the main street and the harbour, older people on the benches, and few people out after dark. Lit windows could account for those indoors.
7. **Sound.** Voices, footsteps on stone and children's play can be emitted from the crowd's position through the existing sound engine.
8. **Approval.** As with the trees and plants, people should enter the game only once their appearance and behaviour have been approved in the testbench.

## 5. Open faults and next steps

The renders made while building the prototype show the following.

- A family in a narrow street sometimes walks pressed against the house fronts, and a carried bag passes into the wall. The formation's slots near a wall need clamping to the clearance, and the right-hand bias should be weaker within a group.
- Too many people gather round the fountain, which serves as a viewing place, a ring of seats and the middle of most stopping places at once.
- A long skirt seen from the front when its wearer sits reads as a dark disc. It needs its own seated shape, falling from the knees.
- The joints are rigid, so knees and elbows show seams at close range. Small sleeves at the elbow and knee, or a skinned version for the nearest people, would hide them.
- There are no steps or slopes in the test scene, and no foot placement on them.
- The hand-holding pose is approximate. The two hands meet only roughly, and the pair do not adjust their spacing to make them meet.
- Faces have no expression and no mouth. A single darker strip for the mouth, and the head nodding while listening, would help at close range.
